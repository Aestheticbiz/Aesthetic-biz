Executive summary

Youthful Balance is honest about being a medical practice. Jennifer Castro is named as APRN with a specific certification from the International Peptide Society, and both Google and Yelp five-star badges are published. Two independent rating sources and a named, certified clinician is more evidence than most practices in this market put on the page at all.

The first screen undoes some of that. On a phone, the headline “Hormone Therapy” breaks in the middle of the word — “Hormon / e / Therapy” — across three lines. The only button reads “Find Out More” and leads to a treatment page rather than an appointment, no telephone number appears before scrolling, and the hero rotates through slides so the message a visitor sees depends on when they arrive.

Evidence limitation: this review covers the homepage on desktop and mobile plus publicly visible information. Treatment-page depth and technical performance were not assessed, so the score is normalised across the six categories that were. Nothing here uses private analytics, and no traffic or revenue figures have been estimated.

What the practice is already doing well

The credibility work here is genuinely done. Several recommendations below are about moving it to where it can be seen.

  • The clinician is named with a specific certification. (Observed) Jennifer Castro, APRN, certified in peptides by the International Peptide Society. A named practitioner with a named certifying body is far more persuasive than “our experienced team”, and most practices never get this far.
  • Two independent rating sources are published. (Observed) Google and Yelp five-star badges both appear. Two independent sources read as considerably more credible than one, and most sites show neither.
  • The positioning is medical and unambiguous. (Observed) “Medical Center”, hormone therapy, peptides. There is no attempt to soften this into a day spa, which is the right call for the patient who is making a clinical decision.
  • Contact details are complete and consistent. (Observed) The North Military Trail address and (561) 537-0537 match across the site — more often wrong than right, and it matters for local search.

The evidence

Priority opportunities

1 · Mobile

The headline breaks in the middle of a word.

Evidence: At 390×844 the hero headline renders as “Hormon”, then “e” alone on its own line, then “Therapy”. The word is split across three lines with a single letter stranded in the middle.

Why it matters to a full-fee patient: This is the largest text on the page, and it is the first thing a patient sees on the device most of them are using. A broken word reads as a site nobody has looked at recently, and a visitor may reasonably extend that impression to the practice.

Recommended: Reduce the headline size on narrow screens so the word fits, or set it to break between words rather than within them. This is a small change in the theme's mobile settings, not a redesign.

2 · Conversion

The only button asks the visitor to read more, not to be seen.

Evidence: The single call to action on the first screen is “Find Out More”, which links through to a hormone replacement therapy page. No telephone number appears on the first screen, and no online booking or enquiry form was found from the homepage.

Why it matters to a full-fee patient: A visitor who has already decided is offered more reading instead of an appointment. Since every enquiry ultimately has to become a phone call, the number missing from the first screen removes the one action the practice actually wants.

Recommended: Make “Book a Consultation” the primary button with the telephone number beside it as a tap-to-call link. Keep “Find Out More” as a secondary link for the visitor who is genuinely still reading.

3 · Message

The hero rotates, so no two visitors see the same thing.

Evidence: Navigation arrows on both sides of the hero indicate a rotating carousel. The slide captured during this review led with “Hormone Therapy” and the supporting line “Feel Younger. Live Better.”

Why it matters to a full-fee patient: A rotating hero means the practice cannot know which message any given patient saw. Visitors also learn to ignore anything that moves, so the slides after the first are seen by very few people — the effort of building them mostly goes unread.

Recommended: Choose the single most valuable message and hold it still. Whichever treatment brings the best patients should own that space permanently rather than sharing it on a timer.

4 · Trust placement

The best evidence on the site sits below the decision.

Evidence: Jennifer Castro's name and certification, and the Google and Yelp rating badges, all appear in lower sections of the homepage. None appear on the first screen.

Why it matters to a full-fee patient: Proof only works where doubt happens. A patient weighing up whether a hormone or peptide practice is credible has to scroll past that judgement to reach the evidence that would have settled it.

Recommended: Put the clinician's name, credential and photograph in the hero, with the two rating badges beside the booking button. Nothing new needs writing — it needs moving up.

5 · First screen

A language selector occupies the top of the page.

Evidence: A dropdown showing a United States flag and the word “English” sits above the logo, at the very top of the mobile screen, followed by a full-width “MENU” bar.

Why it matters to a full-fee patient: Between the language selector, the logo and the menu bar, roughly a third of the first screen is spent before the patient learns anything about the practice. If the site does not in fact offer other languages, the control costs space and returns nothing.

Recommended: If translation is genuinely used, move the selector into the menu. If it is a default from the theme, remove it and give the space back to the message.

Patient decision psychology

PrincipleIn plain EnglishWhat the site shows
Familiarity
Jacob's Law		People expect a site to behave like the others they use.A full-width blue “MENU” bar is unusual, though at least unmistakable. The rotating hero is a pattern most sites have abandoned.
Reachability
Fitts's Law		Important actions should be big and easy to thumb.The “Find Out More” button is small and outlined rather than filled, so it neither draws the eye nor invites the thumb.
Proximity of proofReassurance has to sit where the doubt is.Two rating badges and a certified clinician exist — all of them below the first screen.
AuthorityReal expertise, visible near decisions.Strong on the page as a whole, absent from the opening screen where the judgement is made.
Decision clarityOne obvious next step.One action is offered, but it is “read more” rather than “be seen”.
Helpful defaultsSensible pre-selections that serve the patient.The carousel chooses for the visitor, and chooses differently each time.
Believability
Pratfall Effect		Balanced, realistic communication is trusted more than perfection.“Feel Younger. Live Better.” is broad. Notes on candidacy, monitoring and who these therapies do not suit would raise credibility considerably in this category.

Limitations & methodology

This review is based on the public homepage of youthfulbalance.net viewed on 13 August 2026 at 1366×768 and 390×844, page source retrieved the same day, and publicly visible business information. Treatment-page depth and technical performance were not assessed and are excluded from the score rather than estimated; the total is normalised across the six categories reviewed (90 points available). The hero rotates, so the slide described here is the one shown during this review and may differ from the one another visitor sees.

No private analytics, Google Business Profile insights, booking data or revenue figures were accessed, and none have been estimated. Findings labelled Observed are directly visible; findings labelled Interpretive are professional assessment and are identified as such. No legal, HIPAA, medical board or clinical compliance judgement is offered or implied — those require professional review. Figures in the growth scenario are illustrative placeholders supplied by this report, not measurements of your practice.
