Executive summary

This is a short report, because there is not a great deal wrong. The desktop first screen answers nearly every question a first-time patient has before they scroll: the region is named in the headline, a five-star row carries “over 1,000 5 star patient reviews”, three reasons to choose the practice sit beneath it, and there are two ways to act — “Book an appointment now” and “Call us at (972) 636-6299”, with the same number repeated in the bar above. The photograph is a real treatment being performed in the actual room, not a stock model.

The one substantial problem is on a phone, and it is not really about the design at all. The cookie consent panel occupies rather more than half the screen — everything below the headline is behind it, including the thousand reviews, the three reasons and both buttons. A visitor arriving on a phone sees a promotional bar, a logo, a photograph, two lines of headline, and then a wall of consent text with three grey buttons. All the persuasion is underneath it.

Evidence limitation: this review covers the homepage on desktop and mobile plus publicly visible information. Treatment-page depth and technical performance were not assessed, so the score is normalised across the six categories that were. Nothing here uses private analytics, and no traffic or revenue figures have been estimated.

What the practice is already doing well

Most of this page is already doing what the rest of this review set is being asked to do. It is worth being explicit about why.

  • A thousand reviews, on the first screen. (Observed) “Over 1,000 5 star patient reviews”, with the star row, directly beneath the headline. A count is far more persuasive than a rating alone, because it answers how many people the rating is built on. Almost no practice in this review set puts this above the fold.
  • Three ways to make contact, all visible at once. (Observed) “Book Now” in the header, “Book an appointment now” in the hero, and “Call us at (972) 636-6299” written out as its own button — with the number repeated in the bar above. The patient who books online and the patient who will only ever telephone are both served without either having to search.
  • The photograph is real, and it shows the work. (Observed) An actual practitioner treating an actual patient in the actual room, and on mobile a clinician in scrubs holding the syringes. This is what the patient is buying. Stock photography of a serene model says nothing; this says the practice does this every day.
  • “Why choose U Med Spa” answers the question directly. (Observed) Three lines — top 1% of injectable practices, board-certified and licensed practitioners, known for natural-looking results. Naming “natural-looking results and facial balance” speaks precisely to the fear that stops most first-time injectable patients from booking.
  • The consent panel offers a genuine choice. (Observed) Customise, Reject All and Accept All are all presented as real options rather than one obvious button and a buried alternative. That is more respectful of the visitor than most, and it is the right instinct — the recommendation below concerns only its size on a phone.

The evidence

Priority opportunities

1 · Mobile

The consent panel covers more than half the phone screen.

Evidence: At 390×844 the cookie panel begins immediately below the second line of the headline and continues past the foot of the screen. The star row, the review count, the three reasons and both the booking and calling buttons are all behind it.

Why it matters to a full-fee patient: Everything this page does well is underneath a grey box. A visitor must read and act on a legal notice before they are allowed to see a single reason to choose the practice — and on a phone, where most aesthetic traffic arrives, that is the entire first impression. The thousand reviews are the strongest asset on the site and no phone visitor sees them until they have dismissed something else.

Recommended: Reduce it to a slim bar across the foot of the screen on mobile, keeping the same three choices. The consent is unchanged; it simply stops standing in front of the page.

2 · Proof

The unverifiable claims are standing next to the verifiable one.

Evidence: “Most Trusted Med Spa in DFW” is the headline, and “Top 1% of injectable practices nationwide” is the first reason listed. Directly between them sits “over 1,000 5 star patient reviews”.

Why it matters to a full-fee patient: The review count is checkable and therefore believable. “Most trusted” and “top 1%” are claims the practice makes about itself with no stated source, and a sceptical reader who discounts one tends to discount whatever is beside it. The strongest asset on the page is being asked to share space with two of the weakest.

Recommended: Either attribute the top 1% claim — by whom, measured how, in what year — or let the review count carry the headline on its own. “Over 1,000 five-star reviews in DFW” is a stronger opening line than “most trusted”, because nobody has to take it on faith.

3 · Trust

The practitioners are described but never named.

Evidence: “Expert treatments by Board-Certified and licensed practitioners” appears in the reasons to choose. No individual clinician is named on the first screen, and the mobile hero photograph of a practitioner carries no caption.

Why it matters to a full-fee patient: For injectables the decision is about a person, not a business. The mobile hero already shows a clinician holding the syringes — the visitor can see who would treat them but not who they are, which invites the question without answering it. Board-certified is a category; a name and a qualification is a person.

Recommended: Caption that photograph with her name and credential, and name the lead injector beside the booking button. The picture is already doing most of the work.

4 · Readability

The star row is nearly the same colour as the page.

Evidence: The five stars beside “over 1,000 5 star patient reviews” are rendered in a pale pink against the off-white background, considerably lighter than the surrounding text.

Why it matters to a full-fee patient: The stars are the visual shorthand that carries the review count — most visitors register the shape before they read the words. At that contrast they are close to invisible on a bright screen or in daylight, so the strongest proof on the page is quieter than the claims around it.

Recommended: Darken the stars to match the body text. A small change to the most valuable element on the page.

Limitations & methodology

This review is based on the public homepage of umedspa.com viewed on 13 August 2026 at 1366×768 and 390×844, page source retrieved the same day, and publicly visible business information. This review assessed the homepage at 1366×768 and at 390 pixels wide, together with publicly visible information. Treatment-page depth and technical performance were not assessed and are excluded from the score rather than estimated; the total is normalised across the six categories reviewed (90 points available). The consent panel behaves differently for returning visitors who have already answered it, and the promotional bar rotates, so the message described here may differ from the one another visitor sees.

No private analytics, Google Business Profile insights, booking data or revenue figures were accessed, and none have been estimated. Findings labelled Observed are directly visible; findings labelled Interpretive are professional assessment and are identified as such. No legal, HIPAA, medical board or clinical compliance judgement is offered or implied — those require professional review. Figures in the growth scenario are illustrative placeholders supplied by this report, not measurements of your practice.
